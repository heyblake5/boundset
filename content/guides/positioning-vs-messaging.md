## H1
Positioning vs messaging explained for startup founders

## DEFINITION (40-60 words)
Positioning is the strategy that decides which customers you are best for and why you beat their other options. Messaging is the set of words that carries that strategy to buyers in headlines and sales calls. Positioning changes rarely and stays internal. Messaging changes often and appears everywhere. Do positioning first.

## KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Positioning is a set of choices. Messaging is how you express those choices to a specific reader.
- Positioning comes first. April Dunford describes the messaging document as something that references positioning.
- Positioning is written for your team. Messaging is written for buyers.
- Positioning changes when the market or product changes. Messaging changes by channel and persona.
- Sharper copy cannot fix unclear positioning. Buyers stay confused because the choice underneath is still unclear.
- A quick test tells you which one is broken. If your team disagrees on who you are for, fix positioning. If they agree but the words fall flat, fix messaging.

## The short answer

Positioning decides what you want buyers to believe about your product. Messaging decides how you say it. Messaging turns the positioning into a headline or a sales opener.

| | Positioning | Messaging |
|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | Who are we best for and why do we win | How do we say that to this reader |
| Audience | Your team | Buyers |
| Made of | Competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best fit customers, market category | Headlines, value statements, proof points, talk tracks, boilerplate |
| How often it changes | Rarely | Often, by channel and persona |
| Who owns it | Founder or head of product marketing | Marketing and sales |
| Typical output | Positioning canvas or strategy grid | Messaging matrix, homepage copy, sales deck |
| Failure looks like | Buyers compare you to the wrong tool | Buyers understand you but are not moved |

## What positioning covers

Positioning covers the strategic choices that make your product the obvious pick for a specific buyer. Most teams use the five components from April Dunford's Obviously Awesome. Together they define the context buyers use to judge you.

- **Competitive alternatives.** What buyers would use if you did not exist.
- **Unique attributes.** What you have that those alternatives lack.
- **Value.** What those attributes let customers do.
- **Best fit customers.** Who cares most about that value.
- **Market category.** The frame that makes your value obvious.

None of these are copy. Nobody puts "competitive alternatives: spreadsheets" on a homepage. They are decisions your team agrees on and refers back to. For the full method, read the complete guide to product positioning.

## What messaging covers

Messaging covers the words you use with buyers. It takes each positioning choice and writes it for a specific reader in a specific place. A good messaging document gives every team approved language to start from, so the site and the sales team say the same thing.

A messaging document usually includes:

- A one line description of the product
- A headline and subhead for the homepage
- Value statements for each buyer persona
- Proof points tied to each value statement
- Messaging for each use case
- Answers to the objections sales hears most
- Boilerplate in short and long versions
- A list of phrases the team should stop using

Dunford writes that she builds a messaging document that references the positioning template and goes a level deeper, with value statements for different personas and boilerplate for marketing and sales.

## Why positioning comes first

Positioning comes first because messaging needs something to express. When teams write messaging without positioning, each writer makes their own strategic choices inside the copy. The homepage picks one category and the deck picks another.

This is why many startups rewrite their homepage every few months and it never gets clearer. Each rewrite changes the words. The choice underneath stays unclear, so the next rewrite starts from zero again.

The order also saves time. Once positioning is settled, a copywriter or designer has a clear brief. Messaging decisions get faster because there is a document to check against.

## How to tell which one is broken

You can tell which one is broken by asking your team a few questions. If people disagree about who you serve or what you compete with, positioning is the problem. If everyone agrees but buyers still do not respond, messaging is the problem.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Where to start |
|---|---|---|
| Team members describe the company differently | Positioning | Agree on the five components |
| Prospects compare you to the wrong competitor | Positioning | Revisit alternatives and category |
| Homepage keeps getting rewritten with no gain | Positioning | Settle positioning, then rewrite once |
| Team agrees but the homepage feels flat | Messaging | Rewrite headlines from the value |
| Sales converts well but the site does not | Messaging | Copy the sales team's language onto the site |
| Each channel uses different language | Messaging | Build one messaging matrix |

## How to turn positioning into messaging

To turn positioning into messaging, take each positioning component and write the buyer facing version for a specific place. Work from the top of the messaging hierarchy down. Start with the line that names the category and value, then add supporting points and proof.

| Positioning component | Messaging it feeds |
|---|---|
| Market category | One line description, homepage headline |
| Value | Subhead, value statements per persona |
| Unique attributes | Feature sections, supporting points |
| Best fit customers | Who it is for section, ad targeting language, use case pages |
| Competitive alternatives | Comparison pages, objection answers, sales talk tracks |

### Write the core line first
The core line names what the product is and the main value in plain words. Every other message should support it. If a headline or email line contradicts the core line, change the line or revisit the positioning.

### Add value statements for each persona
Different buyers care about different parts of your value. A founder may care about time. A finance lead may care about cost. Write one value statement per persona, each tied to the same positioning.

### Attach proof to each claim
Each value statement needs a reason to believe it. Proof can be a product capability, a customer result you can verify or a demo moment. Leave out any claim you cannot back up.

## Where value propositions and taglines fit

Value propositions and taglines are messaging. They come from positioning. A value proposition states the value a specific buyer gets. A tagline is a short line, usually used on the homepage or in brand materials. Both should trace back to the value and best fit customer you chose in positioning.

The positioning statement is the one exception that sits on the positioning side. It is a fill in the blank summary for internal use. Keep it off the homepage.

## FAQ
**Q: What is the difference between positioning and messaging?**
A: Positioning is the strategy. It decides which customers you are best for, and why you win. Messaging is the words that carry that strategy to buyers in headlines and sales calls. Positioning is internal and changes rarely. Messaging is external and changes by channel and persona.

**Q: Which comes first, positioning or messaging?**
A: Positioning comes first. Messaging needs a clear strategy to express. Without positioning, each writer makes their own strategic choices inside the copy and the story drifts between the site and sales calls. April Dunford describes the messaging document as an output that references positioning.

**Q: Is a value proposition positioning or messaging?**
A: A value proposition is messaging. It states the value a specific buyer gets in words they understand. It should follow from the value and best fit customer you chose in positioning. If your value proposition changes the target customer or category, the positioning underneath needs a review first.

**Q: Can good messaging fix weak positioning?**
A: Rarely. Better words still describe an unclear choice, so buyers stay confused about what you are and who you are for. If your team disagrees on the target customer or the category, rewriting the homepage will not help much. Settle positioning first, then rewrite the messaging once.

**Q: Who owns positioning and messaging at a startup?**
A: At most early startups the founder owns positioning, often with product marketing once that role exists. Sales and product need to agree with it. Messaging is usually owned by marketing, with sales giving input on the language that works on calls. One person should make the final call on each.

**Q: How often should messaging change compared with positioning?**
A: Messaging can change often. Teams adjust headlines and emails by channel and persona, and test new versions regularly. Positioning should change only when something it depends on shifts, such as the product or the best fit customer. Frequent positioning changes usually mean the original work was skipped.
